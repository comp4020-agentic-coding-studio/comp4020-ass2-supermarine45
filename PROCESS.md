# Process overview

## What I built
**SLOP4230 Retail Ethanol Economics** 
I developed a course that provides a comprehensive analysis of alcohol consumption and purchasing behaviour. The course treats the bottle shop as an empirical dataset and examines alcohol production and retail pricing through factors such as production costs, category-specific taxation, retail margins, and shelf placement. A series of practical assignments complements these topics, providing students with hands-on experience applying the concepts throughout the course.

Here is a more academic and reflective version. I kept the discussion of the agent's limitations honest, but made the progression of the design process clearer and more suitable for a report.

## The Process

The assignment required a subject that was sufficiently _narrow_to be improbable as a conventional university course, while nevertheless having enough depth to support a coherent 12-week curriculum. I initially drew inspiration from topics featured in previous _Ig Nobel_ Awards and explored several ideas, including domestic insects, capsicum taxonomy, and unmanned flying objects (UFOs). This is reflected in my first prototype, which focused on the effective extinguishing of household insects:

[`954f90e`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-ass2-supermarine45/commit/954f90e).

However, I subsequently recognised that a comprehensive academic course requires sufficient grounding in real-world research. The initial topic therefore presented a significant limitation: much of the content generated by the agent was made-up and lacked an adequate research basis. This also made iterative refinement difficult. Without established literature to evaluate the generated material, my feedback was largely limited to general instructions such as producing "better content", which did not substantially improve the results. I therefore abandoned the initial topic and pivoted to `Retail Alcohol Economics`:

[`dc14976`](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-ass2-supermarine45/commit/dc14976).

From this point, I progressively specified the overall structure of the course, including its pages, assignments, grading scheme, and academic policies. I then used the agent to generate the weekly content and supporting visual material (cite prompt; cite commit).

A central requirement of the assignment was that the curriculum be coherent and avoid generic or low-quality "AI slop". I found that the initial generated content did not consistently meet the required level of academic depth. Consequently, I manually reviewed and revised the weekly material, while also identifying appropriate visualisations and other supporting elements (cite commits; cite prompt).

This iterative process highlighted a limitation of agentic coding: the interface and content generated by the agent were simplistic and required iterative manual refinement.

To further reduce "slop" content, I independently searched for relevant academic literature and theoretical concepts (e.g., WET policy) that could serve as foundations for individual weeks. These sources were then incorporated into the curriculum and used to replace or substantiate material generated by the agent: (cite, cite)

The most challenging aspect of the assignment was thus, maintaining academic coherence. Although the agent was useful for generating an initial structure, it was not sufficiently reliable to perform this process autonomously.

More broadly, I observed that the agent, even when using Sonnet 5 with high reasoning effort, struggled with large and complex prompts, particularly those requiring the simultaneous generation of coherent course content, academic grounding, and visual design. At several stages, I therefore supplemented the agent's output with independent designs from other AI tools. In my experience, Google Gemini Pro was particularly useful for relevant topic that can subsequently be incorporated into the course design.

## Knowing It Was Right Before Accepting It
I considered the prototype ready only after manually proofreading the course content and verifying that the curriculum was coherent as a whole. This process revealed several issues that required manual correction, including:

* a grading scheme that was internally inconsistent (cite);
* images with incorrect sizing or formatting (cite); and
* a course schedule that did not adequately fit a 12-week teaching period (cite).

These checks were necessary to ensure that the generated prototype was internally consistent and apt as a complete university course.
