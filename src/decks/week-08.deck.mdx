## The audit

Take an endcap. Take the aisle directly behind it. Compute both.

The reading did this across four chains and found the aggressively
merchandised stock returned **worse** value on the standard-drink metric
than unremarkable mid-shelf neighbours.

Not equal. Worse.

# Negative, not merely absent

---

## Why "negative" is the stronger claim

- **no correlation** would mean displays are uninformative
- **negative correlation** means they are systematically anti-informative
- the second cannot happen by accident at that sample size
- it implies the display is selected for something other than value

---

## Three framing devices

- **was/now pricing** — anchors against a figure you cannot verify
- **multibuy** — moves the unit of comparison from price-per-drink to
  price-per-transaction
- **category adjacency** — sets a poor ratio beside a good one so the
  pair averages in the shopper's head

Each is legal, ordinary, and effective. None is a discount.

---

## What the display is actually for

On this evidence, an endcap functions less as a discount signal and more
as **a visual argument against doing the arithmetic** — the arithmetic
this course has spent seven weeks building the tools for.

The skill this week teaches is noticing the argument is being made.

---

## The uncomfortable part

Before you compute anything, you will expect the endcap to be better
value.

- almost everyone does
- that expectation is the product being sold
- being wrong here is evidence about the display, not about you
- record it honestly in your working — the lab asks you to

---

## Common errors

- auditing the endcap without auditing the aisle behind it, leaving
  nothing to compare against
- treating a was/now price as a verified prior price
- comparing a multibuy total against a single-unit price
- concluding retailers are lying; the reading claims a selection effect,
  which is a weaker and better-supported claim
