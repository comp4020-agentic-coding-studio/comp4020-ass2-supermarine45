## The problem with litres

| Product | Volume | ABV | Litres |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Full-strength beer | 375 mL | 4.8% | 0.375 |
| Bottled wine | 750 mL | 13.5% | 0.750 |
| Vodka | 700 mL | 40.0% | 0.700 |

Ranked by litres, the wine wins. Ranked by what is actually being bought,
it does not come close.

---

## The unit

**One Australian standard drink is exactly ten grams of pure ethanol.**

Not a glass. Not a serving. A mass.

```
standard_drinks = (volume_ml × abv_percent × 0.789) / 1000
index = standard_drinks / price_dollars
```

0.789 is the density of ethanol at 20°C. The /1000 collapses the
percentage and the ten-gram division into one constant.

# A currency, not a serving suggestion

---

## Why the distinction is load-bearing

- a **serving** is advice about consumption
- a **unit of account** is a denominator for comparison
- this course only ever uses the second sense
- week 11 is where the first sense is dealt with properly, and it is
  dealt with as economics rather than as advice

---

## One wrong conversion, one hundred wrong answers

The scraping exercise asks for 100+ SKUs. If the conversion is wrong:

- every index in the dataset is wrong by the same factor
- the **ranking** may still look plausible, which is the danger
- nothing downstream will announce the error
- week 10's optimiser will happily return a confident wrong answer

Check the conversion on three products by hand before trusting it on a
hundred.

---

## Common errors

- multiplying by ABV as a decimal and by 100 in the same step
- using 0.789 as though it converted millilitres to grams directly
- reading a multipack's total volume as a single container's
- rounding standard drinks before dividing, rather than after
