## The rule, in full

**29% of wholesale value.**

That is the entire mechanism. Not per litre. Not per litre of alcohol.
Per dollar of wholesale value.

The consequences are all in the word *value* — because the thing being
bought, measured in week 2's unit, is not proportional to value.

---

## Two wines, same tax rate

| Per unit | Cask, 4 L, 10.5% | Bottle, 750 mL, 13.5% |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Wholesale value | $2.00 | $20.00 |
| WET at 29% | $0.58 | $5.80 |
| Standard drinks | 33.1 | 8.0 |
| **Tax per standard drink** | **1.8c** | **72.6c** |

The same rate. A **forty-fold** difference in what the drinker pays in
tax to reach the same place.

# The rate is identical. The burden is not.

---

## Why "cheap wine", not "wine"

- the tax is proportional to **price**
- the thing being consumed is proportional to **alcohol**
- the two are only loosely related, and least related at the bottom of
  the market
- so the favoured case is not wine in general — it is specifically wine
  where price per standard drink is already lowest

Week 3 established that cask is exactly that case. The tax compounds the
packaging advantage rather than offsetting it.

---

## A structure, not a mistake

The reading is careful here, and so should you be:

- the tax does exactly what an ad valorem tax does
- nobody drafted it to produce this outcome, and nobody drafted it to
  avoid the outcome either
- the effect is **invisible at the stated rate** and only appears once
  week 2's metric is applied to outcomes
- calling it a loophole describes the result, not an intent

---

## Common errors

- applying 29% to the retail price rather than the wholesale value
- comparing tax per bottle instead of tax per standard drink, which
  reverses the conclusion
- assuming a higher tax bill means a heavier tax burden
- treating the inversion as evidence of bad faith rather than of base
  choice
