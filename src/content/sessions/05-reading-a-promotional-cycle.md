## Before the lab

Bring a short price history — three or more observations, taken on
different dates — for one ready-to-drink product from your catalogue.
Two weeks of casual checking is enough. If you have not been recording
prices, bring whatever your retailer's site shows today plus any
was/now pricing it displays, and say so.

## In the lab

**What elasticity means with four data points.** Properly estimated
elasticity needs volume data nobody outside the retailer has. What you
have is price movement. The week's reading is explicit that the shopper-
side estimate is a lower bound on confidence, not a substitute for the
real thing, and the lab treats it that way: you are estimating a direction
and an order of magnitude, not a coefficient you would publish.

**Structural versus promotional.** A price that returns to the same figure
after a fortnight is a promotion. A price that does not return is a
repricing. Telling them apart from three observations is genuinely hard,
and the honest answer is often "I cannot yet". Say that where it applies —
the reading's own dataset ran eighteen months for this reason.

**Why RTDs specifically.** Cans are the category where promotional pricing
moves most and where the standard-drink count per unit is smallest, so a
discount that looks large in percentage terms moves the index less than it
appears to. Compute both for one promoted product and compare.

**Core reading:** *Price Elasticity of Ready-to-Drink Spirit Cans*
(Journal of Consumer Retail Studies, 2022)

## Afterwards

You leave with one elasticity estimate, a written list of what it assumes,
and a habit of recording prices with dates attached — which week 9's
scrape will need at a scale you cannot do by hand.
