### Before the lab

Visit one liquor outlet — the one attached to your catalogue retailer, if
it has a physical store — and draw its floor plan. Paper is fine. Mark the
entrance, the aisles, the refrigerated section, the endcaps and the
counter. Note where the highest-ABV stock sits and where the cheapest
per-standard-drink stock sits, because they are rarely the same place.

**Conduct.** You are a customer in someone's shop. Do not obstruct aisles,
do not photograph staff or other customers, and stop if you are asked to.
A floor plan sketched from memory in the car park is a perfectly
acceptable substitute and has been used for published work.

### In the lab

**The plan as data.** The week's reading treats floor-plan geometry as a
measurable determinant of throughput rather than a matter of taste.
Distance from entrance, aisle width and sightline length are the three
variables it fits, and all three are things you can pace out.

**Two distances.** Measure the walk from the entrance to the best
index on the floor, and the walk from the entrance to whatever is stocked
in the first three metres. Compute the index of both. The gap between
them is the week's argument in one number: the shopper who does not walk
pays for the convenience, and the price of that convenience is set by the
floor plan rather than by the product.

**Comparing plans.** Overlay the room's sketches. Outlets converge on a
small number of layouts, and the reading argues that the convergence is
throughput optimisation rather than imitation. Decide whether your
evidence supports that.

### Afterwards

You leave with a measured floor plan and two index values attached to two
distances. Week 8 asks what the display at the short distance is doing to
make that index harder to read.
