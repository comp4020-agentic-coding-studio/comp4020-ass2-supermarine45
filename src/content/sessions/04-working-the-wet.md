### Before the lab

Bring wholesale-value estimates for four wine products across the widest
price range your catalogue carries — the cheapest cask you can find through
to the most expensive bottle. Retailers do not publish wholesale value, so
estimate it and be ready to say how. The estimate being rough is fine; the
method being undocumented is not.

### In the lab

**The mechanism.** WET is levied at 29% of wholesale value. That is the
whole rule, and its consequences are entirely in the word *value*. A tax
proportional to price is proportional to price whether or not the thing
being taxed is proportional to price — and alcohol content is not.

**Cents per standard drink.** Calculate WET payable on all four products,
then divide by each product's standard-drink count from week 2. This is
the conversion the lab exists for. Dollars per bottle makes the expensive
wine look heavily taxed; cents per standard drink shows what the drinker
of each product actually pays in tax to reach the same place.

**The inversion.** Somewhere in your four products the ordering flips.
Find it, write down the two products either side of it, and write down the
wholesale figure that put them there. Your policy brief needs exactly one
specific, evidenced claim of this shape, and this is where you get it.

**A caution.** The reading is careful that an inversion in the tax burden
is not the same as an inversion in what anyone should buy. Week 11 is
where that distinction is dealt with properly; for now, note that you are
measuring a tax, not issuing advice.

### Afterwards

You leave with a calculated tax burden per standard drink across four real
products and one identified inversion point — which is the spine of the
WET Policy Brief due in week 6.
