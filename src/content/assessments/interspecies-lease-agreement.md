## The brief

> Draft a binding, reciprocal lease between your household and one
> identified resident species, enforceable by a household that did not
> write it.

Week 1 established the dwelling as a residence built, all along, for more
than one species; the lease agreement asks you to write that fact down in
terms specific enough to be honoured. A lease that grants a resident
species vague "space and respect" is not a lease; it is a sentiment. A
lease that names a specific territory, a specific set of household
obligations (what is left, what is sealed, what is never disturbed), a
specific set of resident-side terms the household can actually verify, and
a conflict-resolution procedure drawn from week 11's reversibility-first
framework for the inevitable night it breaks down, is a document another
household could pick up and follow without you there to explain it. Week
12's argument that deliberate infrastructure outlasts accidental
infrastructure applies here as much as it does to a floor plan: a lease
that has to be re-negotiated from scratch every time it is tested was never
really binding.

## What you submit

A single 3,000-word lease agreement, submitted as a PDF, citing the
course's readings and any outside sources you draw on. No separate
artefact or appendix is expected; the lease stands on its own as the thing
being assessed.

This assessment uses the alternative marking mode: a holistic judgement
against the brief rather than weighted criteria. A precise, enforceable
lease that survives contact with week 11's conflict scenario outweighs a
longer document that never specifies what either party is actually bound to.
