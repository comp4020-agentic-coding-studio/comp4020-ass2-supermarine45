**Core reading:** *Designing for Multispecies Tenancy*
(Journal of Architecture and Ecology, 2023)

The semester closes by asking what a dwelling looks like when the last
eleven weeks' findings are designed in from the start rather than
discovered after construction. The week's reading surveys early
multi-species housing proposals — deliberately placed void gaps, sacrificial
humidity zones, boundary-marking channels built into skirting profiles —
and finds that the deliberate versions require less ongoing negotiation
than the accidental infrastructure most households currently rely on,
since a gap designed for transit does not also need to be discovered,
argued over, and eventually respected by trial and error. This is not a
proposal to build homes for insects instead of people; it is the
semester's central claim restated as an architectural brief: a home was
always built for more than one species, and the only choice is whether
that fact gets designed for or merely tolerated. The interspecies lease
agreement due this week is the individual-scale version of the same
claim.

## Outline

- deliberate versus accidental multi-species infrastructure
- void gaps, humidity zones, and boundary channels as design choices
- why designed accommodation requires less ongoing negotiation
- the semester's claim restated as an architectural brief
