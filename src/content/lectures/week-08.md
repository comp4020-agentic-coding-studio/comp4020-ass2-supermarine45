**Core Theory:** *Visual Merchandising and the Intoxication Index: Behavioral Biases* (Martinez, Behavioral Economics Today, 2020).

## The Lecture

Having mapped the retail floor plan in Week 7, we now zoom in on its specific structural traps. The most crucial psychology trick in any supermarket is the endcap in prominent display shelvings.

Retailers weaponize the endcap to induce cognitive overload, forcing consumers to abandon rational algorithmic calculation in favor of heuristic-based purchasing. We will deconstruct the specific visual merchandising tactics used in these zones: psychological pricing tiers (exploiting the left-digit effect of $19.99 versus $20.00), high-contrast "Clearance" typography, and artificial scarcity tags (e.g., "Limit 2 per customer"). 

The overarching goal of the endcap is visual obfuscation. By artificially signaling a bargain, the retailer successfully pushes tax-inefficient stock with poor ABV-to-dollar ratios (such as mid-tier spirits and heavily packaged RTDs) onto consumers who believe they are maximizing their purchasing power. We will mathematically model this illusion.

## Applied Computational Lab

This week’s lab requires an observational retail audit. You will visit a local supermarket liquor outlet and document the primary promotional endcap facing the entrance turnstile. 

Your task is to manually extract the price, volume, and ABV data from the items featured on this promotional display and calculate their exact standard drink efficiency. You will then compare this "promoted" efficiency against the baseline index of the hidden cask wine we modeled in Week 3. The resulting mathematical delta is what we define as the "obfuscation premium"—the exact financial penalty a consumer pays per 10g of alcohol simply because the product was placed in a high-visibility cardboard cut-out.

## Mandatory Literature

- [Sales impact of displaying alcoholic and non-alcoholic beverages in end-of-aisle locations: an observational study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2014.02.032)
  — Nakamura, Pechey, Suhrcke, Jebb & Marteau, *Social Science & Medicine* 108, 2014, 68–73. 
  This study tracks a full year of product-level sales data for a single store. The authors observe that placing inventory on an end-of-aisle display raised overall sales volume by 23.2% for beer, 33.6% for wine, and a staggering 46.1% for spirits. Crucially, the authors calculate that this physical placement creates a psychological effect equivalent to a 4–9% actual price cut. This is the definitive paper to cite when defending the premise that visual display is not merely aesthetic decoration; it is a measurable, distortive economic vector.

- [Alcohol and marketing](https://www.ias.org.uk/report/alcohol-and-marketing/)
  — Institute of Alcohol Studies. 
  The endcap does not exist in a vacuum; it is the terminal physical node of a much larger psychological apparatus. Read this report to understand the wider marketing framework that conditions the consumer before they even step onto the retail floor plan. Your Week 8 lab audit represents a single localized observation within this massive, heavily funded structural context.
