**Core Theory:** *Public Health Externalities of Optimized Discount Liquor Purchasing* (Roberts & Lin, Journal of Medical Ethics, 2023).

## The Lecture

Throughout this semester, we have treated the retail alcohol market strictly as an exercise in quantitative optimization, economic modeling, and regulatory arbitrage. We have quantified the absolute price floor of fermentation, exploited the WET tax loophole, and scripted algorithms to maximize the ABV-to-dollar index. Week 11 provides a necessary, rigorous pause to examine the bioethical consequences of that optimization.

When retailers and consumers systematically drive down the financial cost per standard drink, the resulting macroeconomic efficiency generates profound negative externalities. This lecture examines the direct correlation between hyper-optimized discount purchasing, population-level morbidity rates, and socioeconomic degradation. We analyze how the structural loopholes we have reverse-engineered throughout the semester disproportionately impact vulnerable demographic cohorts, shifting the financial burden of public health crises onto public health infrastructure. Understanding these externalities is vital, not to moralize our models, but to accurately calculate the full socioeconomic impact of the inventory systems you are designing.

## Applied Computational Lab

In this week's lab, you will step away from price optimization to model societal cost. Using datasets provided by the course, you will calculate the imputed public health expenditure associated with the consumption volume of your Week 10 Event Portfolio. 

Your task is to integrate a "social harm coefficient" into your budget spreadsheet, balancing your financial savings against the externalized morbidity costs of high-efficiency alcohol distribution. This exercise ensures your final capstone portfolio is intellectually comprehensive, acknowledging both the microeconomic savings of tax exploitation and the macro-level externalities documented in our literature.

## Mandatory Literature

- [Surveying the range and magnitude of alcohol's harm to others in Australia](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2011.03445.x)
  — Laslett, Room, Ferris, Wilkinson et al., *Addiction* 106(9), 2011, 1603–1611. 
  This comprehensive national survey measures the tangible harms inflicted by alcohol consumption on individuals other than the drinker. The authors document that 70% of respondents were negatively affected by the drinking of strangers, while 30% suffered harm from someone close to them. This paper provides the precise numerical quantification of the negative externalities discussed in the lecture. Your capstone critical reflection is fundamentally weaker for omitting this empirical reality.

- [Special taxation of alcoholic beverages to correct market failures](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1759-3441.2010.00063.x)
  — Freebairn, *Economic Papers* 29(2), 2010, 200–214. 
  Freebairn outlines the classic market-failure argument for imposing special taxation on alcohol in the first instance. This text establishes the theoretical framework wherein the statistical harms documented by Laslett et al. are translated from mere public health statistics into legitimate economic policy questions. Read this paper to understand the precise boundaries of what market-correction taxes are designed to achieve—and, crucially, what economic limits those policies face when confronted with WET loopholes.
